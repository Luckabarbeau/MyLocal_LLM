PROJECT MERIDIAN — ARCHIVE SECTION 1
INITIAL SYSTEM DEFINITION
======================================================================

Project Meridian began as an experimental thermal-energy storage program intended to investigate long-duration operation of a closed-loop high-temperature circulation system. The initial planning documents considered several possible reactor vessels, pump arrangements, sensor networks, and control strategies.

The final primary test vessel used for the Meridian experimental campaign was designated ASTER-17.

ASTER-17 was fabricated from nickel alloy 718 and had a nominal internal operating pressure of 14.2 MPa.

The lead integration engineer responsible for the final ASTER-17 configuration was Dr. Mira Solen.

The internal calibration key assigned to ASTER-17 during commissioning was:

CERULEAN-47

This calibration key is important. Earlier prototype vessels had keys CERULEAN-31 and CERULEAN-39, but neither was used for the final ASTER-17 campaign.

The project team originally considered naming the final vessel ASTER-12, because the twelfth vessel design had passed several early structural simulations. That designation was abandoned after the design underwent five substantial revisions. The final hardware was therefore registered as ASTER-17.

The vessel body contained three principal thermal zones. Zone A occupied the inlet region, Zone B surrounded the central exchange chamber, and Zone C covered the downstream stabilization section.

During normal operation the inlet fluid entered through a tangential manifold intended to suppress large-scale stagnation regions. Engineers experimented with radial and axial inlet concepts during early design studies, but neither was used in the final campaign.

The initial computational mesh contained approximately 18 million cells. Later simulation work used adaptive refinement and therefore did not maintain a constant number of cells throughout the analysis.

A series of preliminary pressure tests were conducted before the working fluid was introduced. Some documents from this period list pressures of 10 MPa, 12 MPa, and 13.5 MPa. These were qualification tests and should not be confused with the nominal ASTER-17 operating pressure of 14.2 MPa.

An early planning memorandum proposed 15 MPa as the nominal pressure. That proposal was rejected because the pump seals showed unacceptable degradation during accelerated endurance testing.

The final operating procedure therefore retained 14.2 MPa.

The Meridian facility was divided into four operational areas:

1. the thermal loop hall;
2. the control and instrumentation room;
3. the auxiliary cooling annex;
4. the data-processing laboratory.

The control room contained six operator stations, although normally only two operators were present during steady-state experiments.

At the beginning of each experimental run the control system performed a staged initialization process. First, instrumentation channels were verified. Second, pump drive electronics were enabled. Third, the thermal loop was brought to circulation speed. Fourth, the heating system was activated.

The exact timing depended on the experiment, and operators were instructed never to infer experimental start time solely from the heater activation timestamp.

The Meridian archive contains many design alternatives because the program was intentionally exploratory. Only statements explicitly describing the final operational configuration should be interpreted as describing ASTER-17 during the main campaign.

ARCHIVE SECTION 2
PUMP DEVELOPMENT HISTORY
======================================================================

The first circulation concept used two identical centrifugal pumps operating in parallel. Simulation suggested that this configuration would provide adequate flow rate, but hardware tests revealed unstable load sharing.

A three-pump arrangement was subsequently evaluated. Under this proposal, two pumps would operate continuously and a third would remain in reserve. This architecture increased system complexity and was abandoned.

A mechanically coupled dual-impeller unit was also considered. Although compact, it transmitted excessive vibration to the upstream instrumentation rack.

The final circulation system used a single primary electromagnetic pump and one physically isolated backup pump.

The primary pump designation was EMP-4.

The backup unit was EMP-4B.

EMP-4 operated under closed-loop flow control. The target flow varied between experiments, but the controller used the same basic proportional-integral architecture throughout the principal campaign.

Several early documents refer to EMP-3. This pump was installed during initial cold-flow testing but was removed before high-temperature operation.

EMP-3 therefore must not be reported as the final primary circulation pump.

The control software tracked motor power, inferred hydraulic load, inlet pressure, outlet pressure, and estimated flow rate.

During long-duration tests, pump telemetry was sampled at a higher frequency than environmental monitoring channels.

The engineering team discovered that the apparent flow oscillation observed during Test Series 4 was largely caused by a pressure transducer synchronization error rather than a real hydraulic instability.

After synchronization was corrected, the residual oscillation was substantially smaller.

The pump mounting structure was redesigned twice. The first support frame was too flexible. The second was sufficiently stiff but difficult to inspect. The third design became the final support structure.

The pump system contained several temperature sensors used for equipment protection. These should not be confused with the process-temperature instrumentation located around ASTER-17.

ARCHIVE SECTION 3
MATERIALS SCREENING
======================================================================

Material selection for Project Meridian involved an unusually large screening program. Candidate materials included stainless steels, nickel alloys, titanium alloys, ceramic liners, and several experimental coatings.

One early design used a stainless-steel pressure shell with an internal ceramic barrier. Thermal cycling produced microcracks near several geometric transitions.

Another design considered alloy 625. It performed well but was not selected because the project team preferred the fatigue performance of alloy 718 under the expected duty cycle.

ASTER-17 was therefore fabricated from nickel alloy 718.

The nominal composition specifications were maintained according to the project's internal procurement requirements. No attempt should be made to infer the exact heat composition from this archive.

Surface treatment was performed after machining and inspection.

The final inner surface finish specification differed between regions because flow requirements were not uniform throughout the vessel.

Several coupons were retained for post-test microscopy.

A separate experimental insert was made from a ceramic composite. This insert was not a structural pressure boundary and should not be confused with the vessel material.

The archive occasionally describes a hypothetical all-ceramic ASTER configuration. No such full-scale vessel was built.

ARCHIVE SECTION 4
CONTROL PHILOSOPHY
======================================================================

The control architecture used several nested loops.

The lowest-level loops controlled actuator commands.

A supervisory layer coordinated flow, heater power, and safety limits.

A separate monitoring process evaluated sensor consistency and could issue warnings without directly modifying actuator commands.

Emergency shutdown logic was implemented independently from the main optimization controller.

The project deliberately separated safety interlocks from experimental control logic.

A model-predictive controller was studied during simulation work but was not deployed during the main ASTER-17 campaign.

Instead, the operational controller used a combination of proportional-integral control loops and rule-based supervisory logic.

A neural-network controller was proposed in a later research note. It was never connected to ASTER-17.

The archive contains performance plots from this proposed neural controller because researchers evaluated it offline using recorded data. Those plots should not be interpreted as evidence that the controller operated the physical system.

ARCHIVE SECTION 5
THERMAL TEST SERIES OVERVIEW
======================================================================

The Meridian campaign consisted of several test series.

Series 1 verified low-temperature circulation.

Series 2 evaluated heater-control stability.

Series 3 investigated transient flow changes.

Series 4 focused on sensor synchronization.

Series 5 examined thermal stratification.

Series 6 performed the longest continuous high-temperature operation.

Series 7 was planned but cancelled after the project objectives had already been achieved.

The longest individual experimental run occurred during Series 6.

That run lasted 38 hours and 24 minutes.

A planning document had originally proposed a 48-hour run, but the actual completed run was shorter.

Another document mentions a 36-hour endurance requirement. This was a minimum qualification objective rather than the measured duration of the longest run.

During Series 6, thermal equilibrium was approached gradually and the operators intentionally avoided aggressive controller tuning.

Several minor instrumentation warnings occurred, but none required emergency shutdown.

ARCHIVE SECTION 7
SECONDARY COOLING DEVELOPMENT
======================================================================

Several coolant compositions were investigated for the backup cooling circuit.

The earliest laboratory loop used pure deionized water.

A later cold-weather study evaluated a 50:50 water-glycol mixture.

Another proposal suggested a 60:40 ratio.

Neither was adopted for the final backup circuit.

The final auxiliary coolant mixture used during the principal ASTER-17 campaign was:

70 percent deionized water
30 percent propylene glycol

or, equivalently, a 7:3 water-to-propylene-glycol ratio by volume.

The project documentation sometimes abbreviates this as WG-73.

This mixture belonged to the auxiliary cooling circuit and was not the primary process fluid circulating through ASTER-17.

The auxiliary circuit was primarily intended to remove residual heat following experimental operation or during abnormal conditions.

The heat exchanger separating the auxiliary circuit from the main thermal loop was designed so the fluids could not intentionally mix.

A conductivity sensor was installed to detect a possible leak across the exchanger.

No confirmed cross-contamination event occurred during the principal test campaign.

ARCHIVE SECTION 9
SENSOR NETWORK
======================================================================

The ASTER-17 process instrumentation included multiple temperature and pressure stations.

Temperature sensors were identified by the prefix T.

Pressure sensors were identified by the prefix P.

Vibration monitors were identified by the prefix V.

Special diagnostic channels sometimes used the prefix S.

Sensor S-19 was one such diagnostic channel.

During a later review of Series 6 data, engineers determined that S-19 had developed a stable measurement offset.

The identified offset was:

+0.84 engineering units

The correction was applied during post-processing by subtracting 0.84 from the recorded S-19 value.

This sign convention is important.

The sensor read 0.84 units too high.

Therefore:

corrected value = recorded value - 0.84

An earlier preliminary note incorrectly suggested a negative offset of -0.84. That preliminary interpretation was superseded after comparison with the reference instrument.

The final accepted offset was positive +0.84.

The offset did not affect the safety system because S-19 was a diagnostic channel rather than a safety-critical interlock.

The reference instrument used during the comparison had recently been calibrated against the laboratory standard.

Several other channels were inspected but did not show an equivalent stable bias.
